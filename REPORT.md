# Where the three weeks go

A process-mining study of 31,509 consumer loan applications.

---

## Situation

A Dutch bank's consumer credit process takes a median of **19 days** from application to resolution, with a long tail — the slowest tenth take more than 35 days, and the slowest case took 286. Cycle time of that length is expensive twice over: it ties up operational capacity, and it gives applicants three weeks in which to accept a competitor's offer instead.

The bank's own event log records all of it: 1,202,267 events across 31,509 applications and 42,995 offers, over roughly one year. The question put to this analysis was the obvious one — **where do the three weeks actually go, and what would shorten them?**

## Approach

The full event log was loaded into a DuckDB database and modelled as three tables at their natural grain: one row per application, one per offer, one per event. Nothing was filtered at load time, because abandoned work is where delay hides. Every analytical question was then answered in hand-written SQL against that model, and every number in this memo is regenerated by a numbered file in this repository.

Four questions were asked. How long do applications take? Between which steps does the waiting accumulate? How much of the work is done more than once? And is the delay universal, or concentrated in a subset?

Two decisions shaped everything downstream and are recorded in full in [`DATA.md`](DATA.md): what counts as a transition between two events, and what counts as a repetition. Reasonable people could decide both differently, and the numbers would change.

---

## Finding 1 — the process is not slow because the work is slow

Of the **690,035 application-days** the bank processed over the year, **565 days were spent handling applications**. That is **0.08%**.

| | days | share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Waiting, inside a single work item | 351,777 | 51.0% |
| Waiting, between work items | 337,693 | 48.9% |
| **Handling** | **565** | **0.08%** |
| **Total** | **690,035** | 100% |

A median 19-day application receives roughly **26 minutes** of human attention.

This reframes the problem before any of it is solved. Adding staff, or making staff faster, addresses 0.08% of the elapsed time. Whatever is causing three-week cycle times, it is not the amount of work involved.

## Finding 2 — a third of all elapsed time is an automatic timer running out

**10,431 applications (33%) end in cancellation.** They are not one thing:

- **7,932 are cancelled by a single system account** a median of **30.7 days** after the last offer was sent, with a standard deviation of **0.16 days** — under four hours of spread across nearly eight thousand applications. The minimum is 30.01 days. This is an automated expiry.
- **2,478 are cancelled by named staff**, with a standard deviation of 16.4 days. These are ordinary human decisions.

![Time from the last offer to resolution](figures/offer_response.png)

The transitions that end in that automatic cancellation account for **215,677 days — 31.3% of all elapsed time in the process.** It is the single largest object in the log, larger than every genuine bottleneck combined.

And it is time in which nothing happens. For those 7,932 applications, the bank makes its last contact a median of **3.94 days** after sending the offer, then waits **26.8 more days** for the timer. That silence totals **219,479 application-days**.

The bank's chasing behaviour is fixed and short: 71.7% of applications receive exactly **two** call attempts, a median of **3.99 days** apart. Third and later attempts are same-session redials, a median of 0.01 days apart. Contact effectively stops in the first week of a thirty-day window.

## Finding 3 — one day in seven is spent doing something already done

Repetition of the same activity within an application accounts for **100,441 days — 14.6% of all elapsed time**, separate from the expiry clock above.

It concentrates in two loops of similar size which are largely **different populations**: of the 8,559 applications in the offer loop, only 3,143 also run the document loop.

| loop | days | applications | share of that step's time in repeat visits |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Chasing incomplete documents | 38,111 | 8,448 | 95% |
| Re-creating offers | 26,209 | 8,559 | 99% |

The right-hand column is the important one. For both loops, nearly all the elapsed time attached to the step sits in the *second and later* visits, not the first. These are not slow steps — they are steps that get repeated, and the repetition is where the time is.

**Neither loop is straightforwardly waste, and the offer loop may be the opposite.** Applications that generate a second offer are accepted **59.3%** of the time against a population rate of **54.8%**, and cancelled 30.0% against 33.2%. Re-creating an offer is associated with a *better* outcome, not a worse one — plausibly the bank successfully re-pricing to win a customer who would otherwise have lapsed. The same caution as elsewhere applies: reaching a second offer requires the applicant to still be engaged, so some of that gap is survivorship rather than effect.

What can be said without ambiguity is the cost: 26,209 days of elapsed time, and a median gap under half an hour to actually create the replacement. The delay is not in producing the second offer. It is in how long it takes to decide that one is needed.

## Finding 4 — the delay is not universal, but not in the way you would guess

Two questions were asked of the population, and both returned negative results worth recording.

**Loan size does not explain response time.** Applicants took a median of 11.7 days to accept the smallest fifth of offers (€5,000–8,000) and 13.8 days for the largest (€27,000–75,000). A two-day difference across a tenfold range in loan size does not support giving large loans different treatment, which was the intuitive hypothesis and is not supported.

**Cancellation is concentrated — in a mechanism, not a customer segment.** 76% of all cancellations are produced by one automated rule. That is a far more actionable concentration than any demographic split would have been, because a rule can be changed on Monday.

---

## Recommendation

**Do not shorten the offer expiry.** It is the obvious move and it was tested at 7, 14, 21, 25 and 30 days. It loses at every threshold.

Cutting the expiry from 30 days to 14 would reduce median cycle time from 19.1 to 15.5 days and free roughly 127,000 application-days. It would also destroy **6,880 conversions — 39.9% of all accepted applications** — because customers are still accepting well past day 14. In lending terms: **€139.1m of principal (42.7%) and €27.8m of gross interest (42.9%)**. Three and a half days of median cycle time is not worth two fifths of the lending.

**Act on the silent window instead.** The opportunity is not the length of the timer but the 26.8 days of nothing before it fires. 7,932 applications sit untouched for that period and are then closed by a system account without anyone having decided anything. The bank stops trying on roughly day 8 of a 30-day window.

Concretely: **run a randomised trial of one further contact at around day 14** — after the existing two attempts have failed, well before the expiry. Randomised rather than observational, for the reason in the caveats. The cost is one contact per application. The prize is a share of 7,932 applications currently closed without a decision, against an average accepted application of **€18,909** of principal.

**Then ask why a quarter of applications need a second offer.** 8,559 do, at a cost of 26,209 days — and creating the replacement takes under half an hour, so the delay is entirely in the time taken to decide one is needed. Since those applications convert *better* than average, the goal is not to eliminate the loop but to reach the same offer sooner. Why the first offer misses cannot be answered from this log: the offer attributes that would explain it are unusable (see caveats).

---

## Caveats

**The effect of chasing customers harder cannot be estimated from this data.** More call attempts correlate with *lower* acceptance, which inverts the intuitive reading. The likely explanation is confounding: the bank calls a second and third time precisely when a customer is hesitating, so reluctance causes the extra calls rather than the reverse. Nothing in an observational log separates the two. **This is why the recommendation is a randomised trial and not a rollout** — the data justifies testing the intervention, not deploying it.

**Waiting and handling cannot be truly separated.** The log records when activities happened, not how long anyone worked. A five-day gap may be five days of queuing or one day of work started four days late. The 0.08% handling figure is a lower bound on work and should be read as "the process is dominated by waiting", not as a literal productivity measure. A related risk — an application holding two work items at once, which would double-count a gap — was measured and bounded: 99.77% never do, and 0.23% reach two.

**Cancellation semantics are inferred, not documented.** `A_Cancelled` is read as withdrawal or lapse and `A_Denied` as a bank refusal, on the evidence of who performs each and when. The publisher's documentation does not define them. If that reading is wrong, Finding 2 changes.

**Two offer attributes in this log are unusable.** `CreditScore` is 0 for 64.5% of offers — a missing-value sentinel, not a score, which makes the reported mean of 318.6 meaningless. The `Accepted` and `Selected` flags are stamped at offer-creation time, before the outcome could be known, and 30,136 offers are flagged accepted against only 17,228 acceptance events in the log. Neither was used here, and neither should enter a predictive model before being resolved.

**98 applications (0.3%) were still open** when the extract was taken and reach no terminal state. They are excluded from outcome analysis rather than counted as failures.

**Interest is stated gross, not risk-adjusted.** The €27.8m applies the offered rate to the offered principal over the offered term, with no allowance for default, prepayment or funding cost. It overstates the economic loss — though not by enough to change the direction of the recommendation.

**Nine further data-quality issues** are recorded in [`DATA.md`](DATA.md), including a daylight-saving defect that affected 11.3% of applications and was found by a reconciliation assertion rather than by inspection.

## Reproducing this

```bash
python -m procmine.load          # XES -> parquet -> DuckDB, then build the model
python figures/make_figure.py    # regenerate the figure
pytest                           # 37 tests against a hand-verified fixture
```

Then run any file in `analysis/` against `data/processed/loans.duckdb`. Every figure quoted above is produced by one of them; [`DATA.md`](DATA.md) names which, alongside every cleaning decision.
