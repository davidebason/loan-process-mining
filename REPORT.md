# Reducing loan cycle time

A process-mining study of a Dutch bank: 31,509 consumer loan applications, producing 42,995 offers and 1,202,267 recorded events. The log covers **397 days**. A median application takes **19 days with a long tail**: the slowest tenth take more than 35 days, and the slowest took 286.

## Summary of findings

- **A third of all elapsed time is an automatic timer running out**
  Cancellations peak sharply 30 days after the last offer was sent, with a standard deviation of a few hours, and the 7,932 applications forming that peak are all closed by one system account. That is **€133.8m of principal, 23.5% of everything the bank offers**, expiring without a decision. These are also the slowest applications in the process: a median of 31.6 days against 14.8 for the ones that are accepted. The bank attempts contact only within the first **4 days (median) to chase a response to the same offer; the remaining 26 are silent**.
- **The delay is independent of the loan amount**
  Median acceptance time moves only from 11.7 to 13.8 days across a tenfold range of loan size.
- **Re-creating offers and chasing documents account for most of the repeated work**
  Two loops out of forty-nine carry 64% of all repetition and 9.3% of the entire process-time. They are not equivalent: one appears to serve the customer, the other to burden them.
- **Waiting between actions is what is slow, not handling**
  Applications spend 0.08% of their elapsed time with a work item open, roughly 26 minutes each. That is a lower bound on effort, but one so small that even a twentyfold correction leaves work a rounding error against three weeks.

## Summary of recommendations

- **Do not shorten the offer expiry.** 
  Tested at five thresholds, it loses at every one. Cutting 30 days to 14 saves 3.5 days of median cycle time and costs 39.9% of conversions, €139.1m of principal.
- **Act on the silent window instead.** 
  The problem is not the timer's length but the 26.8 days of silence before it fires, during which 7,932 applications are closed without anyone having decided anything.
  **Test one further contact at around day 14**, as a randomised trial rather than a rollout. The observational link between chasing and acceptance is confounded, so the data justifies testing the intervention, not deploying it.
- **Ask why a quarter of applications need a second offer.** 
  Creating the replacement takes under half an hour, so the 26,209 days are spent deciding one is needed, not producing it.
- **Reduce the document-chasing burden on applicants.** 
  It is the largest single loop, and the only one whose repetition shows no offsetting benefit to the bank.

# The approach and its findings

Every question was answered in hand-written SQL against the full event log, loaded into a database with nothing filtered out: abandoned work is where delay hides. Every number below is regenerated by a numbered file in this repository.

Two decisions shaped everything downstream and are recorded in [`DATA.md`](DATA.md): what counts as a transition between two events, and what counts as a repetition. Reasonable people could decide both differently. Findings 1, 2 and 4 do not depend on either choice. Finding 3 does, and `DATA.md` records the two definitions that were tried and rejected.

## Finding 1: a third of all elapsed time is an automatic timer running out

**10,431 applications (33%) end in cancellation.** They are not one thing:

- **7,932 are cancelled by a single system account** a median of **30.7 days** after the last offer was sent, with a standard deviation of **0.16 days**: under four hours of spread across nearly eight thousand applications. The minimum is 30.01 days. This is an automated expiry.
- **2,478 are cancelled by named staff**, with a standard deviation of 16.4 days. These are ordinary human decisions.

![Time from the last offer to resolution](figures/offer_response.png)

The transitions that end in that automatic cancellation account for **215,677 days, 31.3% of all elapsed time in the process.** It is the largest single consumer of time in the process, larger than document chasing, offer re-creation and validation combined.

And no one touches it. For those 7,932 applications the bank makes its last contact a median of **3.94 days** after sending the offer, then waits for the timer. Measured from the last action by **any human** to the automated cancellation, the median is **26.8 days**, totalling **212,886 application-days, 30.9% of all elapsed time in the process**.

**These are also the slowest applications in the process.**

| outcome | applications | median duration |
|---|---:|---:|
| Cancelled | 10,431 | **31.6 days** |
| Accepted | 17,228 | **14.8 days** |
| Denied by the bank | 3,752 | 14.1 days |

An application that succeeds takes about a fortnight. The three-week average is dragged up by applications sitting out a timer on their way to being worth nothing, so the headline cycle time is less a measure of how long the work takes than of how long the failures linger.

The bank's chasing behaviour is fixed and short: 71.7% of applications receive exactly **two** call attempts, a median of **3.99 days** apart. Third and later attempts are same-session redials, a median of 0.01 days apart. Contact effectively stops in the first week of a thirty-day window.

## Finding 2: the delay is universal; the cancellations are not

Two hypotheses about *which* applications are slow were tested, and both were rejected. Recording a rejected hypothesis matters as much as recording a confirmed one: it is what rules an intervention out.

**Loan size does not explain response time.** Applicants took a median of 11.7 days to accept the smallest fifth of offers (€5,000 to €8,000) and 13.8 days for the largest (€27,000 to €75,000). A two-day difference across a tenfold range in loan size does not support giving large loans different treatment. That was the intuitive hypothesis, and it is the one the data rejects.

**Cancellation concentrates in a mechanism, not a customer segment.** 76% of all cancellations are produced by one automated rule, a far more useful concentration than any demographic split, because a rule can be changed on Monday.

## Finding 3: one day in seven is spent doing something already done

Repetition of the same activity within an application accounts for **100,441 days, 14.6% of all elapsed time**, separate from the expiry clock above.

**Forty-nine distinct activity steps repeat at all. Two of them carry two thirds of the repetition.**

| loop | days before repeat visits | of all elapsed time | of all repetition |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Chasing incomplete documents | 38,111 | 5.5% | **37.9%** |
| Re-creating offers | 26,209 | 3.8% | **26.1%** |
| **These two together** | **64,320** | **9.3%** | **64.0%** |
| Validating the application *(third)* | 9,098 | 1.3% | 9.1% |

The concentration is what justifies looking only at these two. The third-placed loop is less than a quarter the size of the second, and the remaining forty-six share what is left. Fixing the top two addresses two thirds of all repeated work; fixing the next ten would add barely a third of that.

The two are also largely **different populations**: of the 8,559 applications in the offer loop, only 3,143 also run the document loop. They are two separate problems affecting two mostly separate groups of customers, not one problem seen twice.

Each of those loops has waiting attached to it, and it matters **which visit the waiting sits before**. The first visit is the application simply arriving at that step, which every application has to do. Everything after it is the loop.

| loop | applications | total waiting | before the **first** visit | before **repeat** visits |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Chasing incomplete documents | 8,448 | 39,990 days | 1,879 | **38,111 (95%)** |
| Re-creating offers | 8,559 | 26,509 days | 300 | **26,209 (99%)** |
| *(for contrast)* Validating the application | 7,897 | 27,091 days | 17,993 | 9,098 (34%) |

The two right-hand columns are the finding. For document chasing and offer re-creation, the step is reached quickly and essentially all the delay is the going-round: 95% and 99% of their waiting sits before a repeat visit. These are not slow steps, they are steps that get repeated, and the repetition is where the time is.

Validation is the counter-example, and it is why the split is worth making. Its 27,091 days look comparable, but two thirds of that is the single wait to reach validation the first time, which no amount of loop-fixing would recover. Ranking these three by total elapsed time would put validation second and imply a rework problem it does not have.

**Not all loops are waste, and these two differ in kind.** Applications that receive a second offer are accepted **59.3%** of the time against a population rate of **54.8%**, and cancelled 30.0% against 33.2%. Re-creating an offer is associated with a better outcome, not a worse one, plausibly the bank re-pricing to win a customer who would otherwise have lapsed. Whether it *causes* one is not knowable here: reaching a second offer requires the applicant to still be engaged, so part of that gap is survivorship. **Chasing incomplete documents is the opposite case: it can signal unclear instructions and bureaucracy shouldered onto the client.**

**The cost, however, is unambiguous:** 26,209 days of elapsed time, and a median gap under half an hour to actually create the replacement. The delay is not in producing the second offer. It is in how long it takes to decide that one is needed.

## Finding 4: the process is not slow because the work is slow

Of the **690,035 application-days** the bank processed over those 397 days, **565 days were spent handling applications**. That is **0.08%**, **roughly 26 minutes per application**.

| | days | share |
|---|---:|---:|
| Waiting, inside a single work item | 351,777 | 51.0% |
| Waiting, between work items | 337,693 | 48.9% |
| **Handling** | **565** | **0.08%** |
| **Total** | **690,035** | 100% |

This figure needs care. The log records when a work item was scheduled, started, suspended, resumed and closed, not what anyone did or for how long. **Handling here is the gap that follows a `start` or a `resume`; everything else is waiting.** Work done before a task is opened or after it is closed leaves no trace, and nothing captures effort spent on this application under some other heading. So 26 minutes is a lower bound, not a measurement of labour.

But the conclusion is robust to the measurement being badly wrong. At twenty times the figure it is 8.6 hours per application, still under 2% of a nineteen-day cycle. No plausible correction makes the amount of work, rather than the waiting between work, the reason applications take three weeks.

# Recommendation

- **Make the document requirements easier to satisfy.** *The safest change first.*
  Of the two large loops, this is the one with no offsetting benefit. Re-creating an offer is associated with a *higher* acceptance rate; chasing documents is associated with nothing except delay, 38,111 days of it. Repetition at that step points at the clarity of the bank's own instructions, not at the applicant.

- **Do not shorten the offer expiry.** 
  It is the obvious move and it was tested at 7, 14, 21, 25 and 30 days. **It loses at every threshold**.
  Cutting the expiry from 30 days to 14 would reduce median cycle time from 19.1 to 15.5 days and free roughly 127,000 application-days. It would also destroy **6,880 conversions: 39.9% of all accepted applications**, because customers are still accepting well past day 14. In lending terms: **€139.1m of principal (42.7%) and €27.8m of gross interest (42.9%)**. The loss shrinks as the threshold rises but never disappears: **19.9% of principal at 21 days, 13.6% at 25**, and 7.9% even at 30.
- **Act on the silent window instead.** 
  The opportunity is not the length of the timer but the 26.8 days of silence before it fires. 7,932 applications sit untouched for that period and are then closed by a system account without anyone having decided anything. That is **€133.8m of principal and €25.3m of gross interest, 23.5% of everything the bank offers**, expiring without a decision. The bank stops trying on roughly day 8 of a 30-day window.

  **Test an additional contact at day 14**, a reminder or a revised offer. At that point 16,727 applications are still open: 40% of them go on to accept anyway, and 52% go on to lapse. Reaching the first group costs a phone call and nothing else; the second is the group worth winning. Day 14 also puts ten days between this contact and the previous one, which is currently the last. As things stand only 10% of these applications receive a new offer, on no apparent rule.

  **Size it as a trial, not a rollout.** Roughly **3,000 applications over two months** detects any effect above three percentage points, against an average lapsed loan of **€16,864**. Below three points the effect is real but undetectable at this volume, so the bank should decide in advance that it will act on three or more.
- **Ask why a quarter of applications need a second offer.** 
  8,559 do, at a cost of 26,209 days, and creating the replacement takes under half an hour, so the delay is entirely in the time taken to decide one is needed. Since those applications convert *better* than average (acceptance 59.3% total as opposed to 54.8%, with cancelled 30.0% against 33.2%), the goal is not to eliminate the loop but to reach the same offer sooner. Why the first offer misses cannot be answered from this log: the offer attributes that would explain it are unusable (see caveats).

# Caveats

- **The effect of chasing customers harder cannot be estimated from this data.** More call attempts correlate with *lower* acceptance, which inverts the intuitive reading. The likely explanation is confounding: the bank calls a second and third time precisely when a customer is hesitating, so reluctance causes the extra calls rather than the reverse. Nothing in an observational log separates the two. **This is why the recommendation is a randomised trial and not a rollout**, the data justifies testing the intervention, not deploying it.

- **Waiting and handling cannot be truly separated.** The log records when activities happened, not how long anyone worked. A five-day gap may be five days of queuing or one day of work started four days late. The 0.08% handling figure is a lower bound on work and should be read as "the process is dominated by waiting", not as a literal productivity measure. A related risk: an application holding two work items at once, which would double-count a gap: was measured and bounded: 99.77% never do, and 0.23% reach two.

- **Cancellation semantics are inferred, not documented.** `A_Cancelled` is read as withdrawal or lapse and `A_Denied` as a bank refusal, on the evidence of who performs each and when. The publisher's documentation does not define them. If that reading is wrong, Finding 1 changes.

- **Two offer attributes in this log are unusable.** `CreditScore` is 0 for 64.5% of offers, a missing-value sentinel, not a score, which makes the reported mean of 318.6 meaningless. The `Accepted` and `Selected` flags are stamped at offer-creation time, before the outcome could be known, and 30,136 offers are flagged accepted against only 17,228 acceptance events in the log. Neither was used here, and neither should enter a predictive model before being resolved.

- **98 applications (0.3%) were still open** when the extract was taken and never reached a terminal state. They are excluded from outcome analysis rather than counted as failures.

- **Interest is stated gross, not risk-adjusted.** The €27.8m applies the offered rate to the offered principal over the offered term, with no allowance for default, prepayment or funding cost. It overstates the economic loss: though not by enough to change the direction of the recommendation.

- **Nine further data-quality issues** are recorded in [`DATA.md`](DATA.md), including a daylight-saving defect that affected 11.3% of applications and was found by a reconciliation assertion rather than by inspection.
